# Who Moved My Cheese by Spencer Johnson is a 96-page children's story that exposed the exact reason I've been stuck for three years.

![r/selfimprovement_books - Who Moved My Cheese by Spencer Johnson is a 96-page children's story that exposed the exact reason I've been stuck for three years.](https://preview.redd.it/who-moved-my-cheese-by-spencer-johnson-is-a-96-page-v0-6v4xo03u5rih1.jpeg?auto=webp&s=8bb001ee33ffd7a5e0a52f6a4ad485949119fd11)

Two mice and two tiny humans live in a maze. They find a huge pile of cheese. The mice eat and stay alert. The humans get comfortable. They build their routines around the cheese. They decorate the walls nearby. They stop exploring. The cheese becomes their identity.

Then the cheese disappears.

The mice don't panic. They saw the supply shrinking. They lace up their running shoes and head back into the maze looking for new cheese. No drama. No meetings about it. They just move.

The humans freeze. They complain. They analyze. They wait for the cheese to come back. They return to the same empty spot every day expecting a different result. One of them eventually ventures out and finds new cheese. The other one stays at the empty station, starving and furious at the unfairness of it all.

That's the entire book. A parable about change told through cartoon mice. I read it in 40 minutes and sat in silence for an hour afterward because I was the human standing at the empty station.

Three years ago something in my life stopped working. A career path that had been reliable. I kept showing up to the same empty spot. Same strategies. Same approach. Same expectations. The cheese was clearly gone but admitting that meant admitting I needed to start over. Starting over meant entering the maze again. The maze is dark and uncertain and I'd forgotten what it felt like to not know where I was going.

Three ideas from a children's fable that hit harder than they had any right to.

**The cheese was always going to move.** Nothing is permanent. Not the job. Not the relationship. Not the income stream. Not the strategy that worked last year. Treating any of it as permanent is the setup for every crisis. The mice stayed ready because they never assumed the cheese would last. The humans built an identity around something temporary and then collapsed when it changed.

**The longer you wait, the harder it gets.** The human who eventually moves almost doesn't. Every day he stays at the empty station, the maze gets scarier in his imagination. The fear compounds. The comfort of the familiar spot, even though it's empty, starts to feel safer than the uncertainty of searching. I recognized this immediately. The longer I avoided change, the bigger the change felt. Not because it got harder. Because my avoidance inflated it.

**What would you do if you weren't afraid?** One of the humans writes this on the wall of the maze. It's the simplest question in the book and the one I couldn't answer honestly for months. Because the answer was obvious. I knew exactly what I would do. I just didn't want to admit that fear was the only thing stopping me. Not circumstances. Not timing. Not logistics. Fear of the maze. Fear of starting over. Fear of not finding anything better than what I lost.

The book is almost embarrassingly simple. No research. No frameworks. No science. Just a story about mice and cheese that somehow describes the exact psychological trap that keeps intelligent adults stuck in situations that stopped working years ago.

96 pages. You'll finish it before lunch. The question it leaves you with will take much longer.
