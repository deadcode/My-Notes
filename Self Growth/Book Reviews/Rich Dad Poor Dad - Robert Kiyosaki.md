# Rich Dad Poor Dad by Robert Kiyosaki taught me that the financial advice my parents gave me was designed to keep me working forever.

My parents told me the same thing yours probably did. Go to school. Get good grades. Get a stable job with benefits. Work hard. Save money. Retire at 65.

Robert Kiyosaki's real father gave him the same advice. He calls him "Poor Dad." Educated. Hardworking. Always broke.

His best friend's father gave him the opposite advice. He calls him "Rich Dad." Dropped out of school. Built businesses. Became one of the wealthiest men in Hawaii.

The difference between them wasn't intelligence or effort. It was how they thought about money. Three lessons from Rich Dad that contradicted everything I was raised to believe.

**Assets put money in your pocket. Liabilities take money out. Most people can't tell the difference.** Your house is not an asset. Your car is not an asset. Both take money out of your pocket every single month. Rich Dad taught Kiyosaki that wealthy people spend their lives acquiring assets: businesses, rental properties, investments, intellectual property. Poor and middle-class people spend their lives acquiring liabilities they think are assets and then wonder why they're always behind. I looked at my own finances through this lens and realized almost everything I owned was a liability. I was collecting expenses and calling them achievements.

**The rich don't work for money. They make money work for them.** Poor Dad worked harder every year hoping for a raise. Rich Dad built systems that generated income whether he showed up or not. This is the core split. One person trades time for money. The other builds something that produces money independently of their time. You can't become wealthy on a salary alone because a salary has a ceiling and your time is finite. Wealth starts when your income is no longer directly connected to your hours.

**Financial literacy is the skill nobody teaches you and the one that matters most.** We spend 16 years in school and never learn how taxes work, how debt compounds, how investments grow, or how businesses are structured. Kiyosaki argues this isn't accidental. The system is designed to produce employees, not owners. Employees pay the most taxes, carry the most debt, and have the least control over their income. Once I started studying basic financial literacy on my own, decisions that used to confuse me became obvious. The information was never hidden. It just wasn't handed to me because the system benefits from my ignorance.

The book is imperfect. Some of the advice is oversimplified. Kiyosaki makes it sound easier than it is. But the mental shift it creates, from "work hard and save" to "build things that generate income while you sleep," is the one lesson that separates people who earn well from people who actually build wealth.

Your parents meant well. They taught you what they knew. This book teaches what they didn't.
