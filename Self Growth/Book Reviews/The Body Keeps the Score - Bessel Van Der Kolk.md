# The Body Keeps the Score by Bessel van der Kolk explained why I carry tension in my body that no amount of thinking has ever been able to release.

I always had a tight jaw. Shoulders pulled up toward my ears. A knot in my stomach before conversations that shouldn't have been stressful. I stretched. I exercised. I told myself to relax. Nothing worked because I was trying to fix a body problem with a brain solution.

Bessel van der Kolk is a psychiatrist who has spent over 30 years studying trauma. His core finding is in the title. Your body stores experiences your conscious mind has moved past. You can intellectually understand that something is over. You can talk about it calmly. You can believe you've processed it. And your body can still be living in it, running the same emergency response it learned years ago, completely independent of what your thinking brain has decided.

The science behind it is what made this click for me. Trauma rewires your nervous system. Your amygdala, the brain's alarm system, gets recalibrated. It starts firing at threats that aren't there. A raised voice that sounds like someone from your past. A specific tone in an email. Being ignored in a group. Your rational brain knows you're safe. Your nervous system disagrees. And the nervous system wins every time because it operates faster than thought.

Van der Kolk explains why traditional talk therapy alone often doesn't resolve trauma. Talking accesses the prefrontal cortex. Trauma lives in the brainstem and limbic system, areas that language doesn't reach. You can narrate your story perfectly and still flinch at the same triggers because the part of your brain holding the memory doesn't speak English. It speaks sensation. Tightness. Heat. Nausea. Hypervigilance.

The practical approaches he covers were what made this more than just a difficult read. Body-based interventions like yoga, EMDR, and neurofeedback work because they access the nervous system directly instead of trying to reason with it. Van der Kolk found that trauma survivors who practiced yoga showed greater reduction in symptoms than those using medication alone. Not because yoga is magic. Because it teaches the body something talk therapy can't: that it's safe to feel.

The line I haven't forgotten: "Being able to feel safe with other people is probably the single most important aspect of mental health." Not being successful. Not being productive. Feeling safe. Everything else is built on that foundation, and without it, nothing you build stays stable.

If you've ever wondered why your body reacts to things your mind says shouldn't bother you, this book has the answer.
