# Freakonomics by Steven Levitt showed me that the real causes behind almost everything I believe are completely different from what I assumed.

![r/selfimprovement_books - Freakonomics by Steven Levitt showed me that the real causes behind almost everything I believe are completely different from what I assumed.](https://preview.redd.it/freakonomics-by-steven-levitt-showed-me-that-the-real-v0-gdaj20nah5ih1.jpeg?auto=webp&s=66cd64773883a4134303e47add38c08830255dad)

An economist and a journalist walk into every uncomfortable question society avoids and answer them with data instead of opinions. That's Freakonomics. It's not a self-help book. It's a thinking book. And it will rewire how you evaluate every claim you hear for the rest of your life.

The core premise is one idea: incentives drive everything. Every human behavior, no matter how irrational it looks on the surface, makes sense once you identify what the person is actually incentivized to do. And the incentives are almost never what you'd guess.

Four findings that broke my assumptions.

**Real estate agents don't work for you.** Levitt analyzed data on home sales and found that when agents sell their own homes, they leave them on the market an average of 10 days longer and sell them for 3% more than their clients' homes. Why? Because the agent's commission on your extra $10,000 is only about $150 for them. Not worth the effort of extended showings and negotiations. But on their own house, that $10,000 goes directly into their pocket. The incentive structure means your agent is financially motivated to close your sale fast, not to close it well. I stopped trusting any advisor's recommendation without first asking: how does this person get paid, and does their incentive align with my outcome?

**Information asymmetry is the most powerful advantage in any negotiation.** Levitt demonstrates this repeatedly. The person with more information wins. Car dealers know what the car cost them. You don't. Doctors know the success rates. You don't. Your employer knows the salary range. You don't. Every negotiation you've ever lost probably came down to the other side knowing something you didn't. After reading this I started treating information gathering as the first step of every major decision. Not opinion gathering. Data gathering. The gap between what you know and what the other person knows is where you get exploited.

**Experts have incentives to keep you afraid.** Levitt examines how fear is manufactured and distributed by people who benefit from it. Crime experts exaggerate danger because fear creates funding. News outlets amplify rare events because fear creates viewership. Safety product companies inflate risk because fear creates sales. The actual data almost always tells a calmer story than the experts presenting it. I started fact-checking fear. Every time someone told me something was dangerous or urgent, I'd look at the actual numbers. Almost every time, the risk was a fraction of what was being advertised.

**Conventional wisdom is usually wrong because nobody checks it.** Levitt's method is simple. Take a commonly held belief. Find the data. See if they match. They usually don't. Everybody "knows" that more police reduce crime. The data is more complicated. Everybody "knows" that good parenting determines outcomes. The data suggests genetics and peer groups matter more than most parenting choices. Everybody "knows" that money buys elections. Levitt found that the winning candidate's spending advantage accounts for less than 1% of the vote difference. The winner wasn't outspending the loser. The better candidate was attracting more money because they were already going to win. Cause and effect reversed.

The book doesn't tell you what to think. It teaches you to stop accepting easy answers and start asking what the data actually says. That single habit, questioning the obvious, has been more valuable to me than any productivity system or morning routine.
