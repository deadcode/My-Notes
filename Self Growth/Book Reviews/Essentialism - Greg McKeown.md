# Essentialism by Greg McKeown gave me one rule that replaced every productivity system I've ever tried: if it's not a clear yes, it's a no.

![r/selfimprovement_books - Essentialism by Greg McKeown gave me one rule that replaced every productivity system I've ever tried: if it's not a clear yes, it's a no.](https://preview.redd.it/essentialism-by-greg-mckeown-gave-me-one-rule-that-replaced-v0-o0i608q8z4fh1.jpeg?auto=webp&s=ed9af7d00d8dd6950ea92b0e892c513707bc2c16)

I used to say yes to everything. Every project. Every meeting. Every favor. Every opportunity that sounded vaguely interesting. My calendar was full. My to-do list was impressive. And nothing important was getting done because everything was competing for the same 24 hours.

Greg McKeown argues that the word "priority" was never meant to be plural. It entered English in the 1400s as a singular noun. The one thing that matters most. We didn't start saying "priorities" until the 1900s, and that's when everything started falling apart. The moment you have five priorities, you have none.

The core of the book is one question applied ruthlessly to every demand on your time: is this essential? Not "is this good?" Not "could this be useful?" Not "will someone be disappointed if I say no?" Is this essential? If the answer isn't a clear, obvious yes, the answer is no.

I started applying this and the first week was painful. A coworker asked me to join a committee. Good cause. Nice people. Not essential. No. A friend invited me to an event I felt lukewarm about. Not essential. No. A client asked for an extra deliverable outside our scope. Interesting work. Not essential to my goals. No.

Three things happened within a month. My output on the things that actually mattered doubled. My stress dropped because I stopped carrying 15 half-commitments. And the people around me started respecting my time more, not less. McKeown explains why: when you say yes to everything, people learn that your yes is cheap. When you say yes rarely, your yes carries weight.

The concept that hit hardest was his idea of the "trivial many versus the vital few." In any list of tasks, roughly 20% will produce 80% of the results. Most people spend their days in the trivial many because those tasks are easier, faster, and come with the small dopamine hit of checking something off. The vital few are harder, slower, and often uncomfortable. They're also the only ones that move your life forward.

McKeown's point isn't about doing less for the sake of doing less. It's about creating space for what matters by eliminating what doesn't. You can't add what's essential until you subtract what isn't.

Short book. Sharp writing. Will make you look at your calendar and realize half of it shouldn't exist.
