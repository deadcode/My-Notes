# Mastery by Robert Greene broke down the exact process that Leonardo da Vinci, Darwin, and Mozart all followed and nobody teaches it.

The path to mastery is not random genius. It is a repeatable three-phase process that Robert Greene spent years pulling out of the lives of history's greatest performers. Da Vinci. Darwin. Mozart. Einstein. The pattern is the same across all of them. And almost nobody is following it.

Phase 1 is the Apprenticeship. Every master begins as a student. Not a passive one. An obsessive one. Greene says this phase should last roughly 5 to 10 years. During this period your only job is to absorb. Watch. Practice. Submit to the process. Da Vinci spent 10 years in Verrocchio's workshop before producing anything of his own. Darwin spent years collecting specimens and observations before forming a single theory. The modern instinct is to skip this phase entirely. To create before you have absorbed. To lead before you have followed. Greene says every person who skips the apprenticeship hits a ceiling later that they cannot explain.

Phase 2 is the Creative-Active. After years of deep absorption something shifts. You start combining existing knowledge in ways nobody has before. You are not inventing from nothing. You are connecting dots that only you can see because of your unique combination of skills and experiences. Mozart did not create music out of thin air. He spent his childhood absorbing every musical tradition available and then recombined them in ways that felt entirely new. Greene says this is the phase where your Life's Task emerges. The intersection of your deepest inclination and your accumulated skill.

Phase 3 is Mastery itself. After roughly 20,000 hours of practice and immersion the master develops what Greene calls intuitive intelligence. The ability to see the whole picture without consciously thinking through it. A surgeon who feels something is wrong before the data confirms it. A chess player who sees the winning move without calculating it. A musician who improvises perfectly in real time. This is not magic. It is pattern recognition so deeply embedded that it operates below conscious thought.

The idea that stuck with me hardest was Greene's concept of the Life's Task. He argues that everyone is born with an inclination toward a specific type of work. Not a job title. A mode of thinking. A way of engaging with the world. Most people abandon this inclination early because parents, schools, and society redirect them toward practical paths. The master is simply the person who found their way back to the thing they were drawn to before anyone told them what to be.

Greene puts it plainly. The future belongs to those who learn more skills and combine them in creative ways. In a world of specialists the person who masters the connections between fields becomes irreplaceable.
